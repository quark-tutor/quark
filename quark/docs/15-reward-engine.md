# Reward engine (concept, needs a deep dive)

A reward engine that partners with the curation engine could buy more learning time and attention. It could also hollow out the learning it's meant to support. This page is a starting point for that discussion, not a design.

Many families have a scarce, powerful currency: screen games, a later bedtime, choosing the weekend activity. The same scarcity that makes a reward powerful makes it risky. If the Wonder Wall becomes the chore a kid does to get to the games, the project has lost.

## What the research says, in two directions

- **Rewards can hollow out the activity.** Paying kids for something they already enjoy makes them enjoy it less, and they stop when the payment stops (the self-determination research of Deci and Ryan is the center of this). Alpha School runs on points for games, and its home version was the part that didn't work.
- **Rewards for effort hold up better than rewards for results.** Roland Fryer's large school incentive experiments found that paying kids for test scores did nothing, while paying them for reading books moved the needle. Kids control effort; they can't directly control results.

## Design rules (proposed)

1. **Reward thinking, never correctness.** Minutes of real engagement (not rushed taps), a check attempted without hints, a question brought to dinner, teaching a sibling something. The tutor already tells thinking from tapping, and the reward engine runs on that signal.
2. **Don't reward what they already love.** Shark week needs no reward for a shark-obsessed kid. Save rewards for the stretch material.
3. **Steady and capped.** A fixed, predictable exchange, such as 20 minutes of real thinking earns 20 minutes of game time, up to a daily cap. No escalating ladders, no streaks that reset, no loss-aversion tricks.
4. **Non-screen rewards too.** Choosing the weekend activity, staying up later, picking dinner. The engine shouldn't be only about games.
5. **Parents hold the keys.** The tutor reports what was earned. A parent unlocks it. The tutor never controls the device.
6. **Treat it as an experiment.** Run some themes with rewards and some without, and measure what kids retain, not just how long they sit. If rewards buy attention but not learning, they're not worth their cost. See [Teaching that tests itself](12-teaching-that-tests-itself.md).
7. **Every guardian agrees.** Rewards are part of the values profile. One guardian can't set up a reward system the other hasn't agreed to.

## Open questions

- [ ] What happens on the day the rewards stop? Can they be withdrawn once a habit stands on its own, or is the damage done early?
- [ ] Which rewards do kids actually care about, beyond games?
- [ ] Per child, or shared so siblings earn together?
- [ ] What's the daily cap, and does it differ on school days, weekends and vacations?
- [ ] How do we keep the Wonder Wall from being seen as the toll booth in front of the games?
