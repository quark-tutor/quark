# Themes: the bank

Written 8 Nov 2026 by Claude Code, stop 1 of `docs/theme-a-week-slice.md`. This is a proposal. Nothing here is
imported or built. Brandon and June pick one theme for week 5 (Monday 23 to Sunday 29 November 2026), or ask for
four more. The themes not picked stay in this file and are offered again on later Saturdays, with new ones.

**A theme is one week** (Brandon, 8 Nov 2026). It has seven topics, one a day, each on the slot it is written for,
in the order below. `lib/plan.ts` asks each week of a plan file for science, art, music, literature, math, and
history or geography, with no subject twice, and every slot fed. So a theme's seven topics are the seven subjects,
one each, and all four themes below use the same slot pattern.

## How each theme sits in week 5

All four put the thread on look, wonder, then, listen, look, then and puzzle, so the 28 short lessons the week also
needs are the same whichever theme is picked: **look 5, wonder 6, listen 6, then 5, puzzle 6**. Those 28 are
picked on Saturday 21 November.

## How the pictures and recordings were found

The search was the same as for the short lessons: the Met (its v1.1 search), the Art Institute of Chicago and
Wikimedia Commons, which also holds the NASA, NOAA, NPS, US Navy and Library of Congress items below. Every item
named here was then fetched through Quark's own adapters (`core/content/sources.ts`). **All 47 came back CC0 or
public domain.** Five were looked at:
- the eclipse photographs show the Earth's curved shadow;
- the Met's prize amphora shows a footrace;
- the foam cups really are shrunk;
- one Verne plate shows the giant telescope;
- the 20,000 Leagues plate first found is an indoor scene, so the plate for that topic is picked when the theme
  is written.

The rule for recordings is the one the short lessons use. A recording is taken only if it is a US government
recording, was published in 1925 or earlier, or was released as CC0 or public domain by the people who made it.
No open recording of Beethoven's Moonlight Sonata, of the Seikilos song or of Debussy's Sunken Cathedral was found
whole.

## The Hawaiʻi standard, as applied here

The standard is Brandon's, 8 Nov 2026. A Hawaiʻi topic is offered only when it clears "would a child who lives here
learn something". It must be written in specifics from sources deeper than an encyclopedia.

None of the four themes is a Hawaiʻi theme, and none has Hawaiʻi in it for its own sake. Two topics have a
Hawaiʻi item, each marked **(Hawaiʻi: your call)** with a swap beside it:
- the Moon's Apollo crews trained on Kīlauea's lava;
- the deep sea's Kamaʻehuakanaloa.

A Hawaiʻi theme is written with June, so it is not drafted here alone (see *For a Saturday with June*, at the end).

## What the topics lean on

These are the school's leanings (decided 22 Oct). Each theme's puzzle is arithmetic in a real setting:
- Greece: 5,000 × 50;
- the Moon: 30 × 7,918;
- the deep sea: 36,000 ÷ 33;
- Antarctica: 80 to −76.

Each theme also has a biography, and one has a myth or a tale. For the younger, each has a picture to describe in
full sentences (the second look) and a plain informational day (wonder).

## 1. Ancient Greece

**Why this one.** It follows Egypt in the Core Knowledge order, and Egypt's own story reaches it. The Rosetta
Stone that the boys met in Egypt's last chapter is carved partly in Greek. The week also ends in Egypt, with a
Greek librarian in Alexandria measuring the whole Earth from a well at Syene. Syene is Aswan, beside the Elephantine
nilometer of Egypt's first week.

| Day | Slot | Subject | Topic (the idea named) | What it teaches |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | look | geography | **A city-state**: mountains and the sea | Greece is mountains and sea, with thousands of islands. The mountains split the land into valleys, each with its own city-state, and the sea joined them, so the Greeks became sailors and traders. |
| Tue | wonder | science | **A lunar eclipse**, and how the Greeks knew the Earth is round | Aristotle, about 350 BCE: in an eclipse of the Moon the Earth's shadow on it is always curved, and travelers going south see new stars rise. Who it helps: sailors steering by the stars. |
| Wed | then | history | **An Olympiad**: the games at Olympia | The games were held every four years from 776 BCE (the traditional date). The first race was one length of the stadium, about 630 feet, and the prize was an olive wreath. The Greeks counted years in Olympiads. |
| Thu | listen | music | **An octave**: Pythagoras and the string cut in half | Pythagoras is said to have found that a string half as long sounds the same note higher, and two-thirds as long a fifth: notes in whole-number fractions. Later writers tell it, so the details are legend. Fractions. |
| Fri | look | art | **Black-figure and red-figure** | In black-figure, figures are painted in a slip that fired black, and the details are scratched through. In red-figure, from about 530 BCE in Athens, the background is painted black, the figures stay the clay's red, and the details are drawn with a brush. |
| Sat | then | literature | **The Midas touch**, as Nathaniel Hawthorne told it (*A Wonder-Book*, 1852) | Midas wishes that all he touches turns to gold, until his food and his daughter Marygold do. Then he washes it away in the river. In Hawthorne the wish comes from a smiling stranger who is never named as a god. A myth, and an idiom. |
| Sun | puzzle | math | **Circumference**: Eratosthenes measures the Earth | About 240 BCE, at noon on midsummer, the Sun shone straight down a well at Syene. In Alexandria, at the same hour, a stick's shadow made a fiftieth of a circle. The cities were about 5,000 stadia apart, so the Earth is about 50 × 5,000 = 250,000 stadia around. |

**Pictures and recordings.**
- **A city-state:** NASA MODIS, *Islands of the Aegean Sea* (2021) and *Greece, Turkey and the Aegean Sea* (2019),
  public domain.
- **A lunar eclipse:** NASA, *Total Lunar Eclipse 2026, New Orleans*, four phases with the curved shadow, public
  domain.
- **An Olympiad:**
  - the race track at Olympia, a photograph, public domain;
  - the Met's Panathenaic prize amphora (Euphiletos Painter, about 530 BCE, CC0), a footrace in black silhouettes.
    The runners are nude, as Greek athletes ran, so the flag check may hold it for your look. The prize vases were
    Athens' games, not Olympia's, and the topic says so.
- **An octave:** Gaffurius's 1492 woodcut of Pythagoras with bells, strings and pipes, public domain. There is no
  recording: a parent plays it on a guitar or ʻukulele, plucking a string open and then held at the 12th fret, its
  middle. The alternative with a recording is Offenbach's can-can, from *Orpheus in the Underworld* (1858), a comic
  opera on the Greek myth of the musician Orpheus (Musopen, public domain).
- **Black-figure and red-figure:**
  - the Met's neck-amphora by Exekias, black-figure, about 540 BCE (chariots and horses), CC0;
  - the Met's amphora by the Berlin Painter, red-figure, about 490 BCE (a man singing to his kithara), CC0.
- **The Midas touch:** Walter Crane's Midas and Marygold (1893) and Arthur Rackham's roses turned to gold (1922),
  public domain.
- **Circumference:** Bernardo Strozzi's *Eratosthenes Teaching in Alexandria* (1635), public domain; Antonio
  Beato's photograph of Elephantine and the hills of Aswan (1870 to 1888, Museo Egizio), CC0.

**Values points.** None marked.
- The Greek gods are kept out of the summaries: the games as a festival at Olympia, and Midas as Hawthorne tells
  it. Egypt's first chapter left Ra unmarked the same way. Marking point 4 would mark all seven days, since a
  theme's points go with every topic.
- Point 20 (war; Not yet until 10 for both boys on live) is kept out: no Troy, Marathon or Persians.

**Close to earlier weeks.** Week 3's displacement and Egypt's levers (Archimedes) are left out. Week 4's
candidate 36, Orion the Greek hunter, would be a Greek myth the week before, if taken.

## 2. The Moon

**Why this one.** It is full on the theme's first two nights, so day 1 can send the boys outside with a parent to
find the dark seas the card showed them. It is also the most open subject there is for pictures: NASA's are all
public domain.

| Day | Slot | Subject | Topic (the idea named) | What it teaches |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | look | geography | **The Moon's seas** | Riccioli's map of 1651 named the dark patches seas: Tranquility, Serenity, Rains. They are dry plains of dark lava rock, the same rock as Hawaiʻi's. A parent's note: the Moon is full tonight. |
| Tue | wonder | science | **A crater** | Rocks from space blast out round holes. The Moon has no air, wind, rain or rivers to wear them away, so it keeps them, while the Earth's are worn down and covered. Who it helps: the craters let scientists read what has hit the Moon. |
| Wed | then | history | **A lunar landing**: Apollo 11 | 20 July 1969: Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin landed in the Sea of Tranquility while Michael Collins circled above. Aldrin photographed his bootprint for a soil test; with no wind or rain, the prints should still be there. **(Hawaiʻi: your call)** Apollo crews practiced on Kīlauea's lava fields and at Kapoho in 1969 and 1970, because that lava is like the Moon's seas. |
| Thu | listen | music | **The sustain pedal**, and Debussy's Clair de lune | The piece sounds soft and blurred because the pianist holds the right pedal, which lifts the dampers so the notes ring into each other. This is week 4's candidate 26, moved here: if you pick the Moon, leave it out of week 4 on Saturday. |
| Fri | look | art | **A figure seen from behind**: Friedrich's *Two Men Contemplating the Moon* | Caspar David Friedrich (about 1825 to 1830) paints two men from behind, so we look at the crescent Moon with them. A picture to describe. |
| Sat | then | literature | **Science fiction**: Jules Verne's *From the Earth to the Moon* (1865) | A Baltimore club fires three men at the Moon from a giant cannon in Florida. Apollo 11, 104 years later, also carried three men from Florida, and came home by splashing down in the Pacific, as Verne's sequel had his travelers do. |
| Sun | puzzle | math | **Distance**: about thirty Earths to the Moon | The Moon is about 238,900 miles away on average and the Earth about 7,918 miles wide: 30 Earths in a row reach it. The Moon is about a quarter as wide as the Earth. Multiplication into division, and a quarter. |

**Pictures and recordings.**
- **The Moon's seas:** Riccioli's 1651 Moon map, public domain.
- **A crater:** Apollo 11's photograph of Daedalus crater on the far side (58 miles across), and the Lunar
  Reconnaissance Orbiter's Tycho, public domain.
- **A lunar landing:** Aldrin's bootprint (NASA, 1969); Lovell and Haise training at Kīlauea (NASA, 1970); public
  domain.
- **The sustain pedal:** *Clair de lune*, Laurens Goedhart, 2011, public domain. The alternative is Beethoven's
  Moonlight Sonata, named not by Beethoven but by a critic after his death. It has no open recording on Commons,
  so a parent would play it.
- **A figure seen from behind:** the Met's Friedrich, CC0.
- **Science fiction:** the 1866 Hetzel illustrated edition (Polona's scans on Commons), for example the giant
  telescope Verne's club builds on a Rocky Mountain peak to watch the shot; public domain.
- **Distance:** NASA's 2015 photograph from a million miles out of the Moon crossing in front of the Earth, public
  domain. The Moon looks a little bigger than its true size there, since it was nearer the camera, and the topic
  says so.

**Values points.** None marked. Point 5 is kept out: no age for the Moon or its craters.

**Close to earlier weeks.** Week 1's "Why the Moon changes shape" (the phases) is not repeated. Verne is also in
the deep sea theme, with a different book.

## 3. The deep sea

**Why this one.** It is the part of the ocean nobody in Hawaiʻi sees, however much time they spend in the water.
The boys' ocean so far is the reef and the surface: week 3's coral and humuhumu, and week 1's whales.

| Day | Slot | Subject | Topic (the idea named) | What it teaches |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | look | geography | **A seamount**: Kamaʻehuakanaloa **(Hawaiʻi: your call)** | A volcano growing under the sea about 22 miles off the Big Island's southeast coast, its top about 3,000 feet under the waves (the depth is checked when written). It was named Lōʻihi until the Hawaiʻi Board on Geographic Names gave it this name in July 2021; the federal board followed in 2023. What the name means, and the telling it comes from, are written with June. **Swap** if the boys know it: the Mariana Trench, the deepest place on Earth. |
| Tue | wonder | science | **Bioluminescence** | Below about 3,300 feet no sunlight reaches, and many animals make their own light by a chemical reaction: to lure food, find a mate or hide. Who it helps: a jellyfish's glowing protein now lets doctors and scientists see inside living cells. |
| Wed | then | history | **A bathyscaphe**: the *Trieste* | 23 January 1960: Jacques Piccard and Don Walsh rode the *Trieste* nearly seven miles down to the bottom of the Challenger Deep, in the Mariana Trench. They were about five hours going down and twenty minutes at the bottom. *Bathy-* means deep, so a bathyscaphe is a deep boat: a word part. |
| Thu | listen | music | **A symphonic sketch**: Debussy's *La Mer* | *The Sea* (1905): Debussy called its three parts sketches, and asked for Hokusai's Great Wave (week 1's) on the first score's cover. The recording is a band playing an arrangement for winds, and the topic says so. |
| Fri | look | art | **Radial symmetry**: Ernst Haeckel's jellyfish | In *Art Forms in Nature* (1904), the zoologist Ernst Haeckel drew sea creatures exactly: a jellyfish is the same all the way round its middle, like a wheel. A picture to describe. |
| Sat | then | literature | **A league**: Verne's *Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Sea* (1870) | Captain Nemo's electric submarine, the *Nautilus*. Twenty thousand leagues is how far the *Nautilus* travels under the sea, about 50,000 miles, not how deep it goes: no sea is that deep. |
| Sun | puzzle | math | **Pressure** | Every 33 feet of seawater adds the weight of all the air above us again. At the bottom of the Challenger Deep, about 36,000 feet down, that is about a thousand times (36,000 ÷ 33). Foam cups tied outside a deep-diving submarine came back shrunk to the size of thimbles. |

**Pictures and recordings.**
- **A seamount:** the National Park Service's photograph of pillow lava from Kamaʻehuakanaloa (taken from 1,180
  meters down) and NOAA's map of its slopes, public domain. For the swap, NOAA's map of the Marianas Trench Marine
  National Monument, public domain.
- **Bioluminescence:** NOAA's photograph of a dragonfish's glowing lure, and *Okeanos Explorer*'s goosefish, a kind
  of anglerfish, with its lure; public domain.
- **A bathyscaphe:** the US Navy's *Trieste* beside the USS *Lewis* over the Marianas Trench on the morning of the
  dive, and Walsh and Piccard inside it; public domain.
- **A symphonic sketch:** *La Mer*, I, "From dawn to noon on the sea", the United States Air Force Band's Concert
  Band, public domain (US government); the 1905 cover with the Great Wave, public domain.
- **Radial symmetry:** Haeckel, *Discomedusae*, plate 18, public domain.
- **A league:** the 1871 Hetzel edition's plates by Alphonse de Neuville and Édouard Riou. There are 113 on Commons
  from the Bibliothèque nationale de France, public domain. The plate (for example, the walk on the seabed) is
  picked when the theme is written.
- **Pressure:** a photograph of foam cups shrunk on *Trieste II*'s 1971 and 1972 dives, public domain. Those dives
  recovered film from a spy satellite, which the topic can leave out.

**Values points.** None marked.
- Points 5 and 6 are kept out: no ages, no life's beginnings at hot vents, and nothing of Haeckel's work on
  evolution, only his drawings.

**Close to earlier weeks.**
- Week 3's countershading: hiding by glowing is its cousin made of light.
- Week 3's Mauna Kea measured from the sea floor.
- Week 4's candidate 28 (Grieg's Morning Mood, music that paints a picture) is close to *La Mer*'s idea.

## 4. Antarctica

**Why this one.** It is the one place on Earth most unlike home: a whole continent of ice, where no one lived until
scientists came. And it has the best true adventure there is for a 7- and a 9-year-old, Shackleton's, in which
every man came home.

| Day | Slot | Subject | Topic (the idea named) | What it teaches |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | look | geography | **An ice sheet** | Antarctica is about one and a half times the size of the United States, and almost all of it lies under ice more than a mile thick on average. It gets so little snow that it is a desert. No one lived there before scientists came. |
| Tue | wonder | science | **A huddle**: emperor penguins | The only bird that breeds on the ice in the Antarctic winter. The father keeps the egg on his feet under a fold of skin for about two months without eating, and thousands huddle together, taking turns at the cold edge. Who it helps: each other. |
| Wed | then | history | **An expedition**: Shackleton's *Endurance* | In 1915 the ice trapped and then crushed the ship. The men camped on the ice and sailed small boats to Elephant Island. Shackleton and five others sailed 800 miles in an open boat to South Georgia for help, and every one of the 28 came home (1916). The wreck was found in 2022. A biography. |
| Thu | listen | music | **The banjo**, and the one Shackleton's men kept | When the crew left the ship with only a few pounds of belongings each, Shackleton had Leonard Hussey's banjo kept, and it was played in camp to keep spirits up. The story is as the men told it, and is checked when written. |
| Fri | look | art | **Scale**: Frederic Church's *The Icebergs* (1861) | Church sailed among icebergs off Labrador and painted this huge canvas. Later he added a broken ship's mast in front, which shows how big the ice is. It is northern ice, not Antarctic, and the topic says so. |
| Sat | then | literature | **A fairy tale**: Hans Christian Andersen's *The Snow Queen* (1844) | Gerda walks all the way to the Snow Queen's palace to bring back her friend Kai, whose heart a splinter of a magic mirror froze. Her warmth melts it. |
| Sun | puzzle | math | **A negative number**: below zero | In the South Pole's winter it averages about 76 below zero (°F). The coldest ever measured on the ground was 128.6 below, at Vostok Station on 21 July 1983. From 80 above to 76 below is 156 degrees. Measurement and data; for the younger, a thermometer and a bar graph of two places by month. |

**Pictures and recordings.**
- **An ice sheet:** NASA's Blue Marble view of Antarctica, public domain.
- **A huddle:** NOAA's emperor penguins with chicks, and the National Science Foundation's diagram of the penguin's
  year, public domain.
- **An expedition:** Frank Hurley's photographs of *Endurance* in the pack ice and under sail (1915), public
  domain.
- **The banjo:** *Infanta March*, played on the banjo by Fred Van Eps (Edison, 1914, the year *Endurance* sailed),
  public domain; a 19th-century American banjo at the Met, CC0. No photograph of Hussey's banjo was found.
- **Scale:** Church's *The Icebergs* (Dallas Museum of Art), public domain. The alternative is Friedrich's *The Sea
  of Ice* (1823 and 1824), a ship crushed by ice the way *Endurance* was, public domain. It is not used here because
  the Moon's art day is a Friedrich.
- **A fairy tale:** Edmund Dulac's *The Snow Queen* (1911), public domain.
- **A negative number:** the US Antarctic Program's photograph of the South Pole's winter site manager on his daily
  walk outside, 2020, public domain.

**Values points.** None marked.
- Shackleton is chosen because no one died: Scott's party, who did, are left out.
- No one lived in Antarctica, so point 17 (explorers and colonization) does not arise.
- Point 15 is kept out: no claims about the ice melting.

**Close to earlier weeks.**
- Week 2's floating ice (density) and week 1's snowflakes are not repeated.
- Week 4's candidate 47 (Fahrenheit's thermometer) is close to the puzzle, if taken.

## For a Saturday with June: what a Hawaiʻi theme could be built on

These are not offered as one of the four, because a Hawaiʻi theme week is written with June and not drafted after
the fact. These sources go deeper than an encyclopedia and are open:
- **The Hawaiian Volcano Observatory:** founded on Kīlauea's rim in 1912 by Thomas Jaggar. Its US Geological
  Survey photographs and records are public domain.
- **The Hawaiian-language newspapers on Chronicling America:** eyewitness reports of eruptions and earthquakes, and
  moʻolelo printed in installments.
- **Named tellings:** Nathaniel Emerson's *Pele and Hiiaka* (1915) and the Fornander collection (1916 to 1920),
  each named as the telling used.
- **Pictures:** the Honolulu Museum of Art's CC0 works, as week 2's quilt and kapa used.

A week built from these, with lines from what June and the boys already know, would meet the standard. A week
built from an encyclopedia would not.
