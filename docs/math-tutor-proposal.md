## Decided (Brandon, 9 November 2026, 16:48)

"swap puzzle with math, and rotate tiles daily."

The reason is in the boys' own use, 26 October to 9 November on live (days each first card was answered, of 16
offered, and minutes spent):

| | Look | Wonder | Listen | Then | Puzzle |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| The older boy: days | 11 | 11 | 8 | 7 | 7 |
| The older boy: minutes | 71 | 71 | 42 | 32 | 29 |
| The younger boy: days | 11 | 11 | 11 | 10 | 10 |
| The younger boy: minutes | 45 | 53 | 42 | 27 | 32 |

Use falls with a tile's place on the wall: Then gets as little as Puzzle. The older boy lingers on the first two
and often does not reach the last two. A sixth tile at the end would be used least by the boy who needs it most.
So Math takes the Puzzle slot, and the tiles rotate so each one has its turn at the front.

## The shape, in five lines

1. **One problem at a time**, about something the boys have seen in Hawaiʻi.
2. **When a boy is stuck, the tutor does what you do**: says it another way, slows down to one step, ties it to
   something he knows.
3. **The younger boy runs ahead.** His problems make him slow down and find the sum hidden in the words.
4. **The older boy gets his number practice inside the talk**, a few sums at a time, and guessing does not pay.
5. **You can see where each boy stands**, and you are told early if the older is slipping behind.

## What a boy sees

The Puzzle tile becomes **Math**. There are five tiles, as now. The order of the five changes each day, so no
tile is always last (see *Decided*).

He opens it and gets one problem, read aloud if he wants, with a picture where one helps. For example:

> The drive from home to the beach is 12 miles. Dad drives about 30 miles an hour. Sets of waves come in every 4
> minutes. How many sets come in while you are driving there?

He answers by typing, tapping a number pad, or holding Talk and saying it. Then:

- **Right:** "That's it." The tutor shows the whole working in two lines, so he sees it laid out. A second part of
  the same problem may follow (three or four parts at most), then he is done for the day.
- **Wrong, or stuck:** see below.

About five to ten minutes. No timer, no score, no streak, no points (rule 4).

## When he is stuck or wrong

A wrong answer does **not** earn a clue toward the answer. That is the change from the other cards, and it is for
the boy who guesses to get out of the work.

The tutor's moves, in this order:

1. **"How did you get that?"** He says or types how he worked it out. Talking it through is the practice. What he
   says is kept for you to read.
2. **The same question, said another way.** "It takes 24 minutes to drive there. A set comes every 4 minutes. How
   many sets is that?"
3. **One small step.** "First: how many minutes is the drive?" Then the next step, and the next.
4. **Something he knows.** "It is like counting how many songs play on the way: each one is 4 minutes."
5. **A person.** If the steps run out, the card ends at "ask Mom or Dad", as every card does, and your page shows
   the working so you can pick it up.

The tutor never gives the answer (rule 3). Each step is small enough that he can do it.

## How it is kept safe and correct

- **Everything the tutor says is written on Sunday, not on the spot**: the problem, two other wordings, the steps,
  and the tie to something he knows. All of it passes the same check as every card before a boy sees it (rule 5).
- **The numbers are checked by code, not trusted from the model.** The answer to every part is worked out by the
  program, and a problem whose written answer does not match is held back.
- **The model's only live job is the one it already has**: reading what the boy said and returning a verdict (right,
  a slip in the sum, the wrong sum chosen, a guess). The verdict picks which of the written moves comes next. The
  model writes no words a child sees.
- **Every problem passes the Hawaiʻi question**: has a child here seen this? Surf, tides, the drive, the pool,
  musubi, the market: yes. Thunder, snow days, subway stops: no.

Later, when the tutor can talk live (its own project), it does these same moves better, in its own words. This
proposal does not wait for that.

## Each boy at his own pace

### The ladder (a draft for Brandon and June to mark up)

A list of **what** to learn and in what order, in plain words, with no teaching method attached. Each boy has his
own place on it. He never sees the list, a grade or a level.

| # | Rung | The older boy | The younger boy |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Add and subtract up to 20, quickly | done | done |
| 2 | Add and subtract up to 100 | done | done |
| 3 | Hundreds, tens and ones | done | done |
| 4 | Add up to 1,000 | working | next |
| 5 | Subtract up to 1,000 | next | |
| 6 | Multiplying as "groups of" | working | done |
| 7 | Times tables: 2s, 5s, 10s | working | done |
| 8 | Times tables: 3s and 4s | | working |
| 9 | Times tables: 6s to 9s | | |
| 10 | Division as sharing | | |
| 11 | Division as the times tables backwards | | |
| 12 | Leftovers (remainders) | | |
| 13 | Two-step problems | | |
| 14 | Multiply bigger numbers (3 × 40, then 3 × 42) | | |

**Everyday math, running alongside** (a problem from here a couple of times a week): telling time and how long
something takes; money; measuring length, weight and how much a container holds; reading a bar graph; halves,
quarters and thirds of real things; shapes.

The starting places are Brandon's, 9 November 2026: both boys add and subtract to 100 and know ones, tens and
hundreds; the younger has the small times tables, the older is just starting them and addition to 1,000 in class;
neither has division; "everything else assume no experience" (not since the summer).

### A new rung is taught before it is asked

The first time a boy meets a rung, the tile shows **one worked example** with a picture ("12 musubi, 4 plates:
one on each plate, then another… each plate gets 3"), then gives him his own problem with different numbers in a
different setting. Showing how it is done on another example is not giving his answer (rule 3).

### Finding the right level, by the numbers

Brandon, 9 November: "the math module is something that should be easier to quantitatively zero in on the zone of
proximal development than the other cards." Every math problem ends one of three ways, and each says where the
boy is:

- **Right at the first try:** he already has it. Too many of these and the problems are too easy.
- **Right after a rewording or a step:** this is the zone, what he can do with help. Most of his problems should
  land here.
- **Ends at a person:** too far. The rung is beyond him for now.

So the rule is simple and the same for both boys:

- **He moves up** a rung when he is right at the first try on most of that rung's problems over several days.
- **He stays** while he needs the moves, and the rung comes back in a new setting a few days later.
- **He slides back** a rung, with nothing said, if problems keep ending at a person, or if he trips on something
  marked as known (the summer gap).
- The exact counts ("most", "several") are set in the first build and tuned from the boys' real results.

**The younger boy** can climb as fast as he gets things right. His problems lean on words that hide the sum, and
on saying why. A few quick sums are mixed in because he likes them. **The older boy** gets shorter sums, more of
them, folded into problems about things he cares about, with the talking step first.

## What you see

On the parents' page:

- **Each day:** the problem, whether he got it at the first try, which move got him there, and what he said.
- **Each week:** one line a boy. "Right first time on 5 of 7. Division as sharing is firm. Two-step problems still
  need the steps."
- **Where each boy stands on the ladder**, next to where the school expects a child of his grade to be by June.
- **A plain flag** if the older boy has not moved on something in three weeks, so falling behind is seen in weeks,
  not at the next report card.

## What would be built, in order

1. **The tiles rotate daily.** A small job of its own, and it can go out first: it helps Then as well as Math.
2. **The Math tile, in the Puzzle slot**: one problem a day for each boy, with its other wordings and steps written on Sunday, the
   numbers checked by code, and the "how did you get that?" step. Each boy's place on the ladder is set by your
   hand. The cost is measured here before anything else is added.
3. **The ladder moves itself**, and the parents' page shows where each boy stands, with the flag.
4. **The live tutor does the moves in its own words** (part of the Tutor project, when it gets there).

## Settled, 9 November 2026

1. **Division as sharing starts alongside the first times tables.** Yes.
2. **One problem a day of three or four parts, five to ten minutes.** Yes.
3. **The working is shown after a right answer.** Yes.

One thing the first build does more simply than this page says: the label on what a boy said (a slip, the wrong
sum, a guess) is recorded and shown to the parents, and the moves run in their fixed order. The label choosing the
move comes in the next build, once there are real answers to tune it on.
