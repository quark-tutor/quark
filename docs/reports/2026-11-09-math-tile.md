**Decided at this stop (Code's recommendation, taken by Brandon's words above).**

1. **How a problem is kept: on the chain of layers.** A part is a layer: each child gets a card per part (3 or 4),
   each with its question row, so the wall, the nightly lines, the week page and the handoff keep their paths. The
   shared idea rows for the slot are written in code, one per part, saying only "the day's math, one problem a
   child", with no model call, because a math problem is each boy's own and `ideas` has no child. The card's body
   keeps its fields (title, the setting as text, the part's question, the handoff) and gains a `math` field: the
   part's answer, unit, expression and rung, the two other wordings, the steps (each a question, an answer and an
   expression), the tie, the working's two lines, and on part 1 the worked example when the rung is new to him. The
   question row's accepted answer is the number. `attempts` gains `move` (which move the answer was to: the
   question, how, another way, a step) and `label` (the judge's label on what he said).
2. **Where a place is kept: `skills` and `mastery`.** `skills` takes the ladder, one row a rung and one for each
   everyday area, with a key, a position and the rung's rule (`skills` is in the schema and unused). `mastery`
   gains one column, `place` (done or working); no row is "not yet". Its `p_known` and `attempts` stay untouched for
   slice 2. "Met" is worked out, not kept: a boy has met a rung when it has a place or he has answered a part on it.
   So sharing and every everyday area start new for both boys, and the working rungs (met in class) do not.
3. **The moves, in order, for one part.** Wrong → "How did you get that?" (any real answer taken and kept, no clue)
   → "Here it is another way:" the first other wording → wrong → "One step at a time:" the steps, one by one; a
   right step brings the next, and the last step's answer is the part's answer, so finishing the steps finishes the
   part → a wrong step → "Think of it like this:" the tie, and the part said the second other way → wrong → "This
   one is for a person:". "I don't know" to the question or a step → "Have a guess first."; a reply with no number
   → "Say it as a number." A right answer anywhere → "That's it.", "Here's the whole thing:" and the working, then
   the next part. The look's checks 3 and 4 are worded to this at stop 2.
4. **The counts.** Four parts a problem for each boy: the older, four short sums, one operation each; the younger,
   three where the words hide the sum, then a quick one. The younger's third part asks "How do you know?" after his
   right answer (his "saying why"; any real answer is taken and kept). Everyday problems on days 3 and 6 (Wednesday
   and Saturday). The other five days take his working rungs in turn, in ladder order; sharing rides alongside the
   times-table rung he is working on, on its facts, and takes its turn like a working rung. Every answer is a whole
   number from 0 to 1,000, and money is in whole dollars.
5. **Reading a number, in code.** Digits ("6", "1,000", "$4") or words ("six", "twenty-four", "a hundred and six");
   a unit or "about" is ignored; after "is", "=", "makes" or "equals" the number after the last of them, otherwise
   the first number ("6 sets of 4" is 6; "3 times 8 is 24" is 24); two numbers joined by "or" are not one answer.
6. **The check, `checkMath()`.** Every part's and step's answer equals its expression worked out; the last step's
   answer is the part's; a wording brings in no number the problem does not have (the setting, the question,
   earlier answers, or a number inside the expression); no question, wording, step or tie states the part's answer
   unless it is one of the sum's own numbers; every operation is on one of his done or working rungs (two
   operations only when one is a quick sum to 20); four parts, two to four steps, two wordings, a tie, two lines of
   working, a handoff, and the worked example where the rung is new. Fails once: written again with the problems
   named. Fails twice: every part held back, and the week page says why.
7. **Pictures.** A worked example's picture is drawn by the program from its numbers: dots on plates for groups and
   sharing, blocks of hundreds, tens and ones for adding, a clock face for minutes, bars on a ruler for length, and
   a bar graph from the problem's own numbers. No library picture for math in this slice: nothing in the library
   fits musubi and surf shops, and a drawn picture cannot carry a wrong number.
